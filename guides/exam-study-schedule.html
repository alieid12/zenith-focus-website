How to Make a Study Schedule for Exams

A useful exam timetable is not the one that fills every hour. It is the one you can follow consistently, while leaving room for sleep, breaks and unexpected tasks.

The simple formula: list what you need to learn, rank it by urgency and difficulty, assign focused blocks, then review and adjust your plan each day.

1. Start with exam dates

Write down each exam date and the topics it covers. Estimate how prepared you are for each topic. This turns “I have too much to study” into a list you can work through.

2. Prioritise what needs attention

  • High priority: difficult topics, material you have not reviewed, or subjects with an exam coming soon.
  • Medium priority: topics you understand but need to practise.
  • Maintenance: material you know well but should revisit briefly.

Do not spend all your time on the easiest subject just because it feels comfortable. Give difficult topics time while you can still work on them.

3. Plan focused blocks

Start with 25 minutes of focused work and a 5-minute break, or choose 40–50 minutes if that suits the task better. The exact interval matters less than choosing one clear task and protecting the time.

Before each block, define an outcome: solve five problems, summarise one section, or recall key terms without looking at your notes. “Study biology” is too broad.

4. Schedule real breaks

Stand up, drink water, stretch or step away from your desk. After several blocks, take a longer break. A schedule that ignores rest is difficult to sustain.

5. Use active study methods

  • Answer practice questions before checking the solution.
  • Explain a concept in your own words.
  • Use flashcards for facts and definitions.
  • Revisit material after a gap instead of rereading the same page repeatedly.

6. Make a daily plan you can finish

Choose three important outcomes each day. Put the hardest task in a time window when you usually have good energy. Leave space for tasks that take longer than expected.

Example day:
  • Review a difficult chapter
  • Solve related practice questions
  • Take a longer break
  • Study the next topic
  • Recall yesterday’s material

This is an example, not a rule. Adjust the number and length of blocks to your workload and energy.

7. Review your progress

At the end of the day, mark what you completed, note what remains unclear, and move unfinished work into a realistic slot tomorrow. If you repeatedly miss a task, make it smaller or change its time.

Keep your phone from taking over

Silence non-essential notifications and place distracting apps out of reach. If you use your phone as a timer, start the session and avoid opening unrelated apps during the block.

Start with one session

You do not need a perfect timetable. Pick one topic, define one small outcome and complete one focused session. Repeat, then adjust as you learn what works.
